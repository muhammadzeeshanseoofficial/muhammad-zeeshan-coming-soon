Technical SEO Audit Checklist for Growing Sites

A Technical SEO Audit Checklist is a working order, not a dump of tool warnings.

This guide shows what to inspect first, how to confirm each finding in the browser and in Google Search Console, and when a fix is ready to ship.

Why a Checklist Beats a Raw Crawl Export

A site crawl can produce thousands of rows before you know whether Google can even fetch the important URLs. A checklist forces you to settle discovery and indexation before you debate title tags or image weight.

This order also keeps stakeholders calm. You can show a short list of confirmed issues, each with a URL, a expected fix and a way to re-test, instead of a spreadsheet no one will read.

The goal is not a perfect score in any one tool. The goal is a site that can be crawled, rendered, stored and served without fighting itself.

How to Run a Technical SEO Audit Checklist in a Sensible Order

Start in Google Search Console with Coverage or Pages, robots.txt, sitemaps and the URL Inspection tool. Those reports show how Google already treats the site, which is more useful than a fresh crawl of your own staging copy.

Next, crawl the production host with a tool that respects robots.txt and JavaScript rendering if the templates need it. Compare the crawl to the sitemap and to Search Console, and treat mismatches as the first tickets.

Only then move to on-template items such as canonical tags, pagination, hreflang, Core Web Vitals and structured data. Fixes in those layers fail when the URL is blocked, redirected in a loop or left out of the index.

Close the audit with a re-test plan. Every accepted issue should name the URL pattern, the change, the person who will ship it and the Search Console or crawl check that proves it worked.

  1. 01

    Confirm robots.txt, host, HTTPS and trailing-slash rules.

  2. 02

    Compare index coverage, sitemaps and an independent crawl.

  3. 03

    Resolve status codes, redirect chains and canonical conflicts.

  4. 04

    Check mobile rendering, speed and structured data on key templates.

  5. 05

    Re-test sample URLs and record what changed.

Robots, Host and Protocol Before Anything Else

Open robots.txt and look for accidental Disallow rules on category, article, product or location paths.

A single typo in a Disallow line can keep a whole section out of the crawl, and the page will never have a chance to rank.

Confirm that CSS, JavaScript and font files used to render the template are allowed. If the crawler cannot load the layout assets, the rendered page may look empty even when the HTML source contains the copy.

Check that the live host is the canonical host. Mixed www and non-www versions, leftover HTTP URLs and staging hosts that leak into the index all waste crawl activity and split signals.

If you need a broader primer on how these controls fit together, read What is technical SEO? Before you start changing rules.

Index Coverage and Sitemap Agreement

In Search Console, group pages by why they are not indexed: excluded by noindex, crawled but currently not indexed, duplicate without a user-selected canonical, or blocked by robots.

Each reason has a different fix, so do not treat them as one backlog.

Your XML sitemap should list only canonical, indexable URLs that return a 200 status. If the sitemap still lists redirected, noindexed or parameter URLs, you are asking Google to spend time on the wrong addresses.

Pick a sample of important URLs and run URL Inspection. Live results that differ from the index tell you the current template may have changed, or that a recent fix has not been recrawled yet.

Status Codes, Redirects and Canonical Tags

Soft 404s, empty 200 pages and redirect chains are frequent findings on growing sites. A URL that returns 200 with almost no content can stay in a crawl set for a long time and still fail to rank.

Redirects should go to the final canonical in one hop when you can control the server. Chains through old HTTP, www and retired slugs slow crawlers and sometimes drop parameters you still need.

Canonical tags must agree with internal links, sitemaps and redirects. If the tag points one way and the menu points another, Google may ignore the hint and pick a duplicate you did not intend.

  • Map every 4xx and 5xx on indexable templates.

  • Collapse redirect chains to a single permanent hop.

  • Align rel=canonical, internal links and the sitemap.

  • Remove parameter and tracking URLs from the indexable set.

Rendering, Mobile and Duplicate Templates

If the site is a JavaScript app or uses client-rendered product filters, inspect the rendered HTML, not only the view-source document. A page that looks complete in the browser can still be thin in the snapshot Google stores.

Mobile-first indexing means the smartphone layout is the one that matters. Hidden accordions are usually fine, but content that is omitted from the mobile template, or served only after a user gesture, may never be indexed.

Duplicate templates show up in filtered search pages, printer views, tags and tracking parameters. Decide which URL should represent the topic, then support that choice with links, canonicals and the sitemap.

Speed, Media and Structured Data

Core Web Vitals belong on the audit, but they come after indexation. A fast page that is noindexed still cannot rank, and a slow template that all product URLs share is a better ticket than a one-off blog image.

Look at Largest Contentful Paint, Interaction to Next Paint or Total Blocking Time, and Cumulative Layout Shift on the templates that earn traffic. Fix the shared header, hero image and font loading first, because those files sit on every URL.

Structured data should match the visible page and use a type you can support, such as Article, FAQ, Product or LocalBusiness. Invalid or leftover markup from an old theme is noise, not an enhancement.

How to Prioritise Fixes and Re-Test

Rank issues by how many important URLs they affect and whether they block discovery. A robots typo on the blog folder outranks a single missing alt attribute, even if the tool scores them in the opposite order.

Write each accepted finding as a short ticket: URL pattern, evidence, proposed change, risk, and the re-test. Evidence can be a Search Console screenshot, a rendered HTML snippet or a crawl row, but it should be specific.

After launch, request indexing on a sample, recrawl the section, and only then mark the ticket done. Some fixes need a few crawl cycles before coverage reports catch up, so leave time in the plan.

When the checklist points to a deeper topic, follow it with a focused guide such as Crawl Budget and Indexation Issues or XML Sitemaps and Robots.txt.

Mistakes That Make Audits Unusable

Do not paste a tool export into a slide deck and call it an audit. If a finding cannot be reproduced on a live URL, it is not ready for a developer.

Do not change robots, canonicals and redirects in the same release without a rollback plan. Those levers interact, and a combined release is hard to diagnose when coverage drops.

Do not ignore staging and parameter rules. A noindex that never made it to production, or a faceted URL that the crawler loves, will undo an otherwise clean checklist.

A Worked Example on a Multi-Template Site

Imagine a service brand with a homepage, twelve service pages, a blog and a handful of location pages.

The crawl export lists thousands of parameter URLs from an old search form, while Search Console shows the twelve service pages as crawled but not indexed.

The checklist tells you not to start with image compression. You first remove the search-form URLs from the indexable set, fix a canonical that pointed service pages at the homepage, and only then look at the shared hero image.

After the release, you inspect one service URL, confirm the canonical and the rendered heading, and request a recrawl of the sitemap that now lists only the real service, location and article URLs.

This is what a usable audit looks like in practice: a short narrative, a ticket list and a re-test, not a 40-tab workbook.

Who Owns Each Part of the Checklist

Marketing can pull Search Console, write the issue list and decide which templates matter. Engineering owns robots, redirects, canonical output, rendering and cache headers.

Design owns reserved space for media, font loading and any banner that enters the first screen. Analytics owners should not add a tag that delays the same first screen without a review.

Write names against tickets even on a small team. A checklist without an owner becomes a report that is read once and filed.

If you do not have a developer this week, still finish the diagnosis and the evidence. A clear ticket is easier to brief later than a tool PDF.

What to Put in the Final Audit Note

The note should open with the indexable URL policy in one paragraph. It should then list confirmed issues in priority order, each with a URL pattern, a recommended change and a re-test.

Attach only the evidence that supports those issues. A screenshot of URL Inspection, a robots.txt excerpt or a short crawl filter is enough.

Close with what you will not do yet, and why. That section prevents a well-meaning teammate from shipping a robots change that fights the canonical plan.
