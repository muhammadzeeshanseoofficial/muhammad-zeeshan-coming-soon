What to Expect From an SEO Audit

What to Expect From an SEO Audit is a practical description of the process, the deliverable, and the way you should use the findings.

This guide shows what a serious audit usually includes, what it should not invent, and how to turn the document into work.

What to Expect From an SEO Audit in Plain Terms

What to Expect From an SEO Audit, in plain terms, is a diagnosis, not a ranking package and not a redesign by itself.

The auditor inspects how the site is crawled, indexed, structured and presented, and then explains the issues that matter, with evidence and a suggested order of work.

The output should help you decide what to do next week, not only tell you that SEO is important.

Different audits have different widths, from a technical pass to a wider look at content, local presence and competitive context, so the brief should say which width you are buying.

This site also has a technical audit checklist, which is the method for one part of the work. This page is about the engagement: how it runs, what you should receive, and how to use it.

If you know that, you can brief the work and judge the document you are given. You can also decide whether you need an audit at all, or whether you already have a backlog you should implement.

What You Will Be Asked for

Expect a request for access to the site, Search Console, analytics, and any staging or documentation that would prevent a guess. Expect questions about the offer, the markets, the important pages, and any recent migrations or redesigns.

A serious auditor will not want to invent those facts, and you should not withhold them if you want a useful document. You may also be asked who can ship template or content changes, because recommendations have to land somewhere.

Prepare a short list of what is already known to be broken, and of any legal or brand limits on the copy. If you cannot grant access, say so, and agree what can still be reviewed from the outside.

An outside-only review can still be useful, and it will have limits, which should be written down. The quality of the input is part of the quality of the audit.

What Usually Happens During the Review

The auditor will look at public pages, templates, and often a crawl, alongside Search Console coverage, sitemaps, robots, and a sample of important URLs.

They may inspect rendering, canonicals, internal links, titles, and the structure of key templates, then sample content for intent and uniqueness.

On a local business they may also look at the profile and the NAP consistency, and on a store they may look at categories, products and facets.

They should keep a record of evidence, such as URLs, screenshots, or report excerpts, so a finding can be reproduced.

They should not spend the entire time inside a single tool’s score, and they should not invent problems to fill a slide quota.

If they find something urgent, such as a noindex on the live host, they should say so before the final document, if the engagement allows it.

A good audit is a process of looking, checking, and prioritising, not a dump of every warning a crawler can print. You can ask, mid-way, which areas are already looking like the main story.

What the Deliverable Should Contain

A useful report opens with the situation and the main risks, then lists findings in a priority order, each with evidence, a recommended action, and an owner type such as developer, merchandiser or writer.

It should say what was in scope and what was not, and it should name the date and the environment, such as live or staging.

It should not invent traffic forecasts, guaranteed outcomes, or statistics that were not measured. It may include a short recommended sequence, such as thirty, sixty and ninety days, as a planning aid, not as a promise.

Appendices can hold crawls and tables, but the main story should be readable without them. If you cannot tell, after a first read, what to do first, the deliverable is not yet finished.

Ask for that clarity, because the document is a working brief, not a souvenir. A live walk-through of the findings is often as valuable as the file itself.

How to Use the Audit After You Receive It

Walk the findings with the people who will implement them, and turn the first items into tickets with owners and dates.

Do not try to ship everything at once, and do not leave the file in a folder while you buy a new tool.

Re-test after the first release, and only then move down the list, because some later items will change when the first ones are fixed.

If you asked for a diagnostic before a longer engagement, use the audit to decide the next shape of work, including whether to do it in-house.

If a finding is unclear, ask for the URL and the reproduction steps before you argue about taste. An audit is successful when work begins, not when the PDF looks impressive.

Set a review date, even if it is informal. The document should still make sense on that date, or it should be updated.

A Worked Example of an Audit That Was Used

A services firm commissioned an audit after a redesign, and granted Search Console and site access.

The main findings were a robots rule left from staging, a set of duplicated service titles, and a slow shared template, each shown with URLs and a recommended owner.

The first week of implementation removed the robots rule and requested recrawls, which was the urgent item.

The next weeks fixed titles and reserved space on the shared template, then the firm decided which new pages, if any, were still needed.

They did not start with a content package, because the audit had shown that access and templates were the bottleneck. The document was useful because it was specific and because someone owned the tickets.

That is What to Expect From an SEO Audit when the engagement is healthy. The file and the implementation belong together.

Different Widths of Audit

A technical audit concentrates on access, indexation, rendering, speed and structural issues, and is close to the technical checklist on this site. A content or on-page audit concentrates on intent, uniqueness, titles, internal links and the cluster map.

A local audit adds the profile, NAP, and location pages. A store audit adds collections, products and facets.

A wider commercial audit may also comment on markets and on the way the site supports the offer, without inventing a full business strategy.

Ask for the width you need, and do not assume that a twenty-page file has covered all of these if the brief was narrow.

A narrower, thorough audit is more useful than a wide, shallow one. You can always commission a second width later, with the first already implemented.

What an Audit Cannot Honestly Do

It cannot guarantee a rank, a traffic number, or a date on which a competitor will be overtaken. It cannot see every future algorithm change, and it cannot replace a legal review of your claims.

It cannot implement the work unless implementation was part of the engagement. It also cannot be complete in the sense of listing every minor crawler warning on a large site.

It should be complete in the sense of covering the important patterns in scope, with enough samples to be fair. If a vendor promises completeness and certainty, treat that as a sales claim, not as a professional standard.

Honesty about limits is part of the quality of the audit. You should expect that honesty in the document and in the conversation.

How to Judge the Quality of the Document

Look for specific URLs, reproducible evidence, and a clear order of work. Look for language you can hand to a developer or a writer without a translator.

Look for an absence of invented statistics and of generic advice that could apply to any domain. Look for a match between the brief and the document, including the markets and the important templates you named.

If those qualities are present, you can live with a plain layout. If they are absent, a decorated slide deck will not save the work.

Ask questions until you can restate the main story in your own words. If you cannot, the walk-through is not finished.

Audit Engagement Mistakes

Buying an audit you have no one to implement is a mistake, unless you are also planning that capacity. Refusing access and then complaining that the findings are shallow is a mistake.

Asking for a free audit and expecting a full, evidenced diagnostic is usually a mistake of expectation, or a sales process in disguise. Filing the document without tickets is a mistake that wastes the fee.

Treating every crawler warning as equal is a mistake the auditor should help you avoid, and that you should not reintroduce in the tickets.

Commission the audit when you are ready to use it, or ready to decide on the next engagement.

Timing is part of the value. A good document on a shelf is still a shelf.
