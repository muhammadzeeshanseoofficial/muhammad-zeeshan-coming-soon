Header Tags and Page Structure That Match Intent

Header Tags and Page Structure decide how a visitor scans a page and how clearly the topic is organised.

This guide shows how to use one H1, useful H2s and supporting H3s so the page matches intent and stays easy to read.

Why Heading Structure Matters

Most visitors do not read a page in a straight line. They scan the headings to decide whether the page will answer their question, and only then do they read a section.

Search engines also use headings to understand the outline of a page. A clear outline is easier to interpret than a wall of bold text that is not a heading at all.

Good headings help accessibility too. People who navigate by headings should be able to move through the page in a sensible order.

When the heading structure is messy, even good writing is harder to trust. The page feels unfinished before the first paragraph is read.

How to Use Header Tags and Page Structure Together

Header Tags and Page Structure work together when each heading introduces a real section and the sections follow a path a person would recognise. Do not skip from an H1 to an H4 because the design looks smaller.

Keep one H1. It is the title of the page, and it should agree with the title tag and the primary intent.

Multiple H1s are usually a sign that the template is repeating a site-wide heading or that two pages have been merged.

Use H2s for the main parts of the answer or the offer. Use H3s for steps, variants or questions inside an H2.

If you need H4s often, the section may be trying to cover too much.

The heading text should still make sense if you exported it as a table of contents. If a heading only says Part two or More, rewrite it.

Match Headings to Intent

A definition guide can open with what the topic is, then how it works, then a process, then mistakes and questions.

A service page can open with the offer, who it is for, how the work runs and what happens next.

Look at the questions people actually ask. If they ask about cost, timing, coverage or eligibility, those questions can be headings, as long as you can answer them honestly.

Do not invent a heading for a topic you will not explain. An H2 that promises a case study or a price list and then waves the subject away damages trust.

If two intents need two heading sets, you may need two pages. A single outline that tries to serve a buyer and a researcher often serves neither.

How to Write the Words in a Heading

Write headings in language a client would use. Title Case is a style choice on this site, but the words themselves should still be plain and specific.

Include the primary topic in the H1 and in at least one H2 when it is natural. Other headings can use related wording so the outline does not sound like a slogan repeated eight times.

Keep headings short enough to scan, but long enough to be specific. Process is weak.

Avoid cute or vague headings that hide the content. A person who is in a hurry should be able to jump to the right section from the heading alone.

Design Choices That Help or Hurt the Outline

A heading has to look like a heading and be a heading in the HTML. Styled paragraphs or divs that only look large will not give you a reliable outline.

Do not use a heading tag only because you want a colour or a size. If the line is not the title of a section, style it another way.

Accordions and tabs can still contain headings, but the important unique content should remain in the HTML, not only in a closed component that is hard to reach.

A table of contents is useful on a long guide when it is generated from the real H2s. A hand-written toc that does not match the page will be noticed.

Service, Blog and Location Templates

Service templates should not force the same H2s onto every offer if the offers differ. A shared structure is fine, but empty or cloned sections should be removed.

Blog templates should leave room for a unique outline. If every article uses the same four headings, the design is writing the page instead of the author.

Location pages can share a pattern, such as the service, the area, the proof you can support and the next step, as long as the heading text and the body are genuinely local.

Product or category headings should describe the group the visitor is in, not a marketing slogan that could sit on every collection.

A Worked Example on a Mixed-Intent Page

A page titled Header Tags and Page Structure opens with an H1 about typography, then an H2 about brand colours, then a long block of copy with bold lines that look like headings.

The rewrite keeps one H1 about heading structure, adds H2s for levels, intent, writing style, design mistakes and a checklist, and turns the bold lines into real headings or ordinary sentences.

A colour discussion is moved to a design document or a different page, because it is not the intent of this URL. Internal links point readers to the on-page checklist and to the title-tag guide.

The table of contents now matches the page, and a screen-reader list of headings tells the same story as the visual design.

A Heading QA Pass You Can Reuse

Export or list every heading in order. Check that there is one H1, that H2s are the main sections, and that no level is skipped without a reason.

Read only the headings. If you can describe the page accurately, the structure is working.

If you cannot, rewrite the wording or the order.

Confirm that the primary topic appears in the H1 and in a meaningful H2. Then confirm that no two pages share the same H1.

Common Heading Mistakes

Using headings to style buttons, navigation or footer columns makes the outline noisy. Keep heading tags for section titles.

Repeating the exact primary keyword in every H2 makes the page harder to read and does not look confident. Be specific instead.

Leaving the theme’s default H1, such as Welcome or Blog, on an important URL is a wasted signal and a poor first line for visitors.

Splitting one thought across two headings with almost no text between them creates a table of contents that promises more than the page delivers.

Headings, Accessibility and Jump Links

People who navigate by headings, and people who use a table of contents, depend on unique and descriptive heading text. Duplicate Click here headings are a problem in both cases.

Give important H2s a stable id when you build jump links. Changing the id later will break bookmarks and internal links.

Do not rely on colour alone to show a heading. Size, weight and the heading tag should work together.

If you hide a heading visually, ask whether it should exist at all. Visible structure is usually the better choice for visitors and for search.

Where Headings Fit in the Wider On-Page Pass

Headings are the skeleton. The title, the body, the media and the links still have to do their jobs once the skeleton is right.

Use the On-Page SEO Checklist after the outline is settled, and How to Write SEO Content That Ranks when the body needs more work.

On a large site, agree a heading pattern for each template and then allow writers to change it when the intent demands it. A rigid outline is only useful when the pages are truly alike.

Review headings again after a redesign. Themes often reintroduce multiple H1s or turn section titles back into styled paragraphs.
